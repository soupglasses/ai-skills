# 06 What law teaches about rules

Background for chapter 05, with no entries of its own. Law has the longest record of writing
rules for readers who cannot ask the author what was meant.

**A rule is a proxy for its reason.** Rules are "entrenched generalizations likely to be
under- and over-inclusive in particular cases", and even a well-fitted rule "may yet produce
a bad fit in a particular circumstance neither imagined nor imaginable when the rule was
originally crafted" (`schauer1991`). Expect misfit. Answer it by rewording the rule (5.1),
and keep the goal beside any measure (5.7).

**Words alone do not settle application.** Hart held that rules "have a core of clear
applications surrounded by a penumbra of uncertainty". Fuller replied that no rule can be
applied "without understanding the purpose that the rule was supposed to serve"
(`schauer2008`). A stated reason decides the penumbra (5.3).

**A set of rules can fail as a set.** Fuller's eight requirements are that rules be "general
in scope and application, public, prospective, coherent, clear, stable, practicable, and
there be congruence between official action and declared rule" (`rundle2026`). For an
instruction file:

- general: no rule written for one incident (5.1),
- public: loaded where it applies,
- coherent and clear: no contradiction, one term per thing (5.6, 5.4),
- stable: reworded when it fails, not on every session,
- practicable: the agent has the means to comply,
- congruent: a rule the maintainer does not enforce in review is deleted (5.8).

**Rule or standard.** Law gives content "ex ante (rules) or ex post (standards)". Rules
cost more to write, and standards "tend to be more costly for individuals to interpret"
(`kaplow1992`). Write an exact rule where a mistake is costly or cannot be undone, such as
publishing. State a goal and leave the method open elsewhere.

**Drafting practice.**

- State the rule directly, not by its exceptions (`ofr`). "never use the old-fashioned style
  of proviso" (`scotpco`).
- Split a sentence that holds "both a general rule and 1 or more exceptions", or "an
  unresolved policy issue" (`house1995`).
- "The positive is often easier to understand than the negative", though "A prohibition may
  be best expressed in the negative" (`ukopc2024`).
- Add a purpose clause "only when necessary": it "can become a crutch used to avoid
  resolving difficult substantive issues" (`ofr`).
